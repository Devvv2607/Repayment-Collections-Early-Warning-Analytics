## (a) 60-second pitch

"I analysed 13.6 million loan repayment records from the Home Credit dataset to answer one collections question:
which early-delinquent borrowers should a collections team call first?

The first thing I found was a data trap. The payments table has one row per *payment*, not per installment, and
5% of installments are split across several payments. Summing the scheduled amount naively overstates it by
9.8 billion, about 4.4%. So I rebuilt the data at installment grain in DuckDB SQL, and I assert that grain in
automated checks.

On that clean base, 91.4% of installments are paid on time, and only 1.29% of installments that are 1–30 days
late roll to 31+ days on the next installment. Half of them cure straight away.

Then I tested a 'repeat delinquent' segment: two or more prior late payments, built with a window function that
only looks backwards, so there's no leakage. It's statistically significant but weak: 1.37% vs 1.29%, and it
covers 71% of early delinquents, so it's useless as a queue. What actually separates borrowers is *severity*.
Anyone with a prior installment more than 30 days late rolls at 4.0%, three times the portfolio. That's 10,800
borrowers and 2.6 billion outstanding, about 6.5% of the active book. That's the segment I'd recommend for early
intervention, validated with an A/B holdout.

Everything is reproducible with one command, and it feeds an Excel MIS, a Power BI model and a live web
dashboard."

## (c) 15 likely questions, with crisp answers

**1. Why installment grain and not payment or loan grain?**
The business event is "a scheduled installment was or wasn't paid on time", and DPD and roll rates are defined on
it. Payment grain double-counts the scheduled amount and makes split payments look partial (7.85% of rows look
partial, only 0.018% of installments really are). Loan grain hides the sequence you need for roll rates.

**2. How did you avoid double-counting?**
I collapsed payment rows to `(SK_ID_PREV, NUM_INSTALMENT_VERSION, NUM_INSTALMENT_NUMBER)` and took
`MAX(AMT_INSTALMENT)`, because it is constant within that grain (I verified it: zero exceptions), while
summing `AMT_PAYMENT`, which really is additive. Then `checks.py` asserts that the grain is unique and that the total
scheduled amount equals a `SELECT DISTINCT` route over the source: both 222.20B, against a naive 231.98B.

**3. Why the last payment date for DPD, not the first?**
With split payments, the first payment can be on time while most of the money arrives weeks later. The last
payment is when the installment was fully settled, so it measures real lateness. Example: installment 11 of loan
1083675 got 292 early, 12,267 two days late and 3,461 seven days late, so its DPD is 7, not 0.

**4. Roll rate vs default rate: what's the difference?**
A default rate is a stock or end-state measure ("what % of loans ended 90+ / charged off"). A roll rate is a flow
measure ("of what is in bucket X now, what % moves to bucket X+1 next period"). Roll rates are the leading
indicator that collections teams use to staff and prioritise. Chaining them (0→30→60→90) gives a forward view of
losses long before defaults show up.

**5. Why is your roll rate only 1.29%? Isn't that low?**
In this data, "1-30" is mostly borrowers paying 1–5 days late (median 4). Half cure on the next installment.
It's also an installment-to-installment roll, not a month-end balance roll, and the population is borrowers who
came back to Home Credit, so it skews towards better payers. I reported what the data shows rather than
redefining buckets to make the number bigger.

**6. Why does the repeat-delinquency definition have no leakage?**
The count is a window `SUM(is_late) OVER (PARTITION BY SK_ID_CURR ORDER BY due_day RANGE BETWEEN UNBOUNDED
PRECEDING AND 1 PRECEDING)`. It counts only installments due on **earlier days**: never the current one, never
same-day installments of other loans, never anything later. A prior installment due on day *d* is known to be late
from day *d*+1, which is on or before the current due date, so a collections team would really have had this
feature at decision time.

**7. Why `RANGE` and not `ROWS 1 PRECEDING`?**
`ROWS` counts physical rows. If a borrower has two loans with installments due the same day, `ROWS` would let one
see the other in an arbitrary order: a subtle leak. `RANGE ... 1 PRECEDING` on the day number excludes the whole
current day.

**8. Your segment is significant but you say it's weak. Explain.**
With 700k observations, almost any difference is significant (z = 12). Practical significance is about effect
size and coverage: 1.37% vs 1.29% is a 7% relative lift, and the segment holds 71% of early delinquents. It doesn't
narrow the queue. Severe history (≥1 prior 31+ DPD) gives 3.1× lift on 10.8% of cases. That's an actionable
queue.

**9. Isn't switching to the severe definition "tuning to the target"?**
No. The headline numbers use the original definition exactly as specified, and all thresholds 1–5 are reported. The
severe definition is a clearly labelled sensitivity analysis, also reported at thresholds 1–5. It's a different,
equally simple business rule, and I'd validate it out of time and with an A/B test before adopting it.

**10. How did you test that the gap isn't noise?**
A two-proportion z-test (repeat vs not-repeat, pooled standard error) plus 95% Wald intervals for each group. Repeat:
1.37% [1.35, 1.40]; not-repeat 1.07% [1.03, 1.11]; z = 12.2, p ≈ 5e-34. The intervals don't overlap.

**11. How did you size the opportunity without balances?**
Active loans are `DAYS_TERMINATION = 365243` (Kaggle's "not set" value; 98% of them have an installment due in the
last 60 days, so they really are live). Outstanding ≈ remaining installments × annuity. Severe-history segment:
10,838 borrowers, 13,621 loans, 2.56B outstanding, 6.5% of the 39.66B active book. It's a proxy, and I'd say so.

**12. Limitations?**
(i) DAYS are relative to each borrower's application date, so there are no calendar months, no vintages and no
seasonality. (ii) No month-end snapshots, so roll rates are per installment, not per balance per month. (iii)
Survivorship: these are previous loans of returning applicants, and charged-off loans stop producing installments,
so they leave the roll denominator. (iv) Outstanding is a proxy. (v) 14% of borrowers lack demographic attributes
(test-set). (vi) It's observational: high roll rates show where to act, not that acting works.

**13. What would you do with real bureau or collections data?**
Use month-end account snapshots to build balance-weighted roll rates and vintage curves. Add bureau delinquency
on other lenders as a leading indicator. Join contact history (calls, promises-to-pay, kept promises) to measure
treatment effect. Run a randomised holdout on the early-contact segment. Then possibly build a simple
scorecard, but only after the rule-based segment proves its lift out of time.

**14. Why DuckDB?**
It runs SQL directly on Parquet with no server, and processes 13.6M rows in seconds on a laptop. The SQL is
standard (window functions, `QUALIFY`, `UNPIVOT`), so it ports to Snowflake, BigQuery or Postgres with minor
changes. The whole pipeline is about 3 minutes end-to-end.

**15. How do you know the outputs are right?**
Six automated checks stop the pipeline on failure: unique grain; scheduled and paid totals reconcile to the
source; DPD buckets (and every segment dimension) sum to the total; every roll-matrix row sums to 100%; the 30→60
numerator and denominator agree across three tables; Power BI aggregates reconcile to the fact table. RESULTS.md,
the Excel file and the website are all generated from the same tables in the same run.

## (d) The 3 most important queries, annotated

### 1. Collapsing payments to installment grain (`sql/02_fact_installment.sql`)

```sql
WITH installment AS (
    SELECT SK_ID_PREV,
           NUM_INSTALMENT_VERSION,
           NUM_INSTALMENT_NUMBER,                      -- these 3 columns = one installment
           MAX(SK_ID_CURR)               AS SK_ID_CURR,
           MAX(DAYS_INSTALMENT)          AS due_day,        -- same on every row of the installment
           MAX(AMT_INSTALMENT)           AS scheduled_amt,  -- repeated per payment row: take ONCE, never SUM
           COALESCE(SUM(AMT_PAYMENT), 0) AS paid_amt,       -- payments are additive
           COUNT(AMT_PAYMENT)            AS payment_rows,
           MIN(DAYS_ENTRY_PAYMENT)       AS first_pay_day,
           MAX(DAYS_ENTRY_PAYMENT)       AS last_pay_day    -- settlement day drives DPD
    FROM stg_installments
    GROUP BY SK_ID_PREV, NUM_INSTALMENT_VERSION, NUM_INSTALMENT_NUMBER
)
SELECT *,
       GREATEST(COALESCE(last_pay_day, 0) - due_day, 0) AS dpd,      -- unpaid -> aged to day 0
       paid_amt / NULLIF(scheduled_amt, 0)              AS coverage  -- NULLIF avoids divide-by-zero
FROM installment;
```
*Talking point:* "MAX for the attribute, SUM for the event." That one choice is the difference between 222B and
232B.

### 2. Building the transition pairs (`sql/04_roll_rates.sql`)

```sql
WITH latest_version AS (
    SELECT *
    FROM fact_installment
    QUALIFY ROW_NUMBER() OVER (                       -- keep one row per installment number:
        PARTITION BY SK_ID_PREV, NUM_INSTALMENT_NUMBER
        ORDER BY NUM_INSTALMENT_VERSION DESC          -- the latest (re-scheduled) version
    ) = 1
)
SELECT SK_ID_PREV, NUM_INSTALMENT_NUMBER, dpd_bucket,
       LEAD(dpd_bucket)       OVER w AS next_dpd_bucket,        -- where the NEXT installment lands
       LEAD(dpd_bucket_order) OVER w AS next_dpd_bucket_order
FROM latest_version
WINDOW w AS (PARTITION BY SK_ID_PREV ORDER BY NUM_INSTALMENT_NUMBER);   -- same loan, in order

-- 30->60 roll rate
SELECT COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE next_dpd_bucket_order >= 2) / COUNT(*) AS roll_30_60
FROM roll_sequence
WHERE dpd_bucket = '1-30' AND next_dpd_bucket IS NOT NULL;   -- last installment has no "next": excluded
```
*Talking point:* `QUALIFY` filters on a window result without a subquery. `LEAD` turns a sequence into pairs.
The matrix is just `GROUP BY dpd_bucket, next_dpd_bucket` with a `CROSS JOIN` of buckets so empty cells show 0.

### 3. Leakage-safe history feature (`sql/05_repeat_delinquency.sql`)

```sql
SELECT r.*,
       COALESCE(SUM(is_late::INT) OVER prior_history, 0)    AS prior_late_cnt,    -- headline
       COALESCE(SUM((dpd > 30)::INT) OVER prior_history, 0) AS prior_severe_cnt   -- sensitivity
FROM roll_sequence r
WINDOW prior_history AS (
    PARTITION BY SK_ID_CURR            -- the BORROWER, across all of their loans
    ORDER BY due_day                   -- time order by due date
    RANGE BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING  -- everything before...
          AND 1 PRECEDING              -- ...up to the previous DAY: excludes the current installment
);                                     --    and anything else due the same day
```
*Talking point:* the frame end is the leakage control. Ending the frame at `CURRENT ROW` would count the
installment you are trying to predict from. Ending it at `UNBOUNDED FOLLOWING` would use the future.
